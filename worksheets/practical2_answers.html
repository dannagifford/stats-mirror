Hypothesis testing to correlation

Author

BIOL33031/BIOL65161

This worksheet will help you practice the key ideas behind hypothesis testing — including the null hypothesis, p-values, test selection, and interpretation — using real data and R code.

You’ll apply tests to different types of data (continuous and categorical), interpret p-values, and connect statistical decisions with biological questions.

Part 1: Understanding hypotheses

  1. In your own words, what does the null hypothesis (H₀) represent?
None✅ Answer

It represents the idea of no real effect — any pattern in the data arises from random variation alone.

  1. Suppose you’re testing whether a new antibiotic reduces bacterial growth compared with a control.
    Write out suitable null (H₀) and alternative (H₁) hypotheses.
None✅ Answer
  • H₀: Mean bacterial growth is the same with and without the antibiotic.
  • H₁: Mean bacterial growth is lower when treated with the antibiotic.

2.2 Run a two-sample t-test

Use a t-test to test whether the mean growth differs between treatments.

None✅ Answer 
t.test(OD600 ~ treatment, data = growth)

    Welch Two Sample t-test

data:  OD600 by treatment
t = -6.4475, df = 26.055, p-value = 7.762e-07
alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group Antibiotic and group Control is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 -0.18669203 -0.09643743
sample estimates:
mean in group Antibiotic    mean in group Control 
               0.6826470                0.8242117

If p < 0.05, reject H₀ and conclude the antibiotic significantly reduced growth.

2.3 Check your interpretation

If the p-value is 0.012, what does that mean in context?

None✅ Answer

If there were no true effect, we’d expect data this extreme only 1.2% of the time by chance.
We therefore reject H₀ and conclude there is evidence the antibiotic reduces growth.

2.4 Run a one-tailed t-test

We might instead ask whether the antibiotic specifically reduces growth.

Re-run the t-test using a one-tailed test (hint: use alternative = "less").

None✅ Answer 
t.test(OD600 ~ treatment, data = growth, alternative = "less")

    Welch Two Sample t-test

data:  OD600 by treatment
t = -6.4475, df = 26.055, p-value = 3.881e-07
alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group Antibiotic and group Control is less than 0
95 percent confidence interval:
       -Inf -0.1041185
sample estimates:
mean in group Antibiotic    mean in group Control 
               0.6826470                0.8242117

The one-tailed test places all α in one tail, making it slightly more powerful to detect effects in the predicted direction.

Run a χ² test to check if resistance is associated with species.

None✅ Answer 
chisq.test(tab)

    Pearson's Chi-squared test with Yates' continuity correction

data:  tab

If p < 0.05, there is evidence of an association between species and resistance.

3.3 When should you use Fisher’s exact test?

None✅ Answer

When expected cell counts < 5 or sample sizes are small — Fisher’s test calculates the exact probability of observing the data under H₀.

Part 5: Reflection

  1. What does it mean if a result is statistically significant but not biologically meaningful?

  2. Why do we say we “fail to reject” H₀ instead of “accepting” it?

  3. What steps could you take to increase the power of an experiment?

None✅ Example answers

  1. What does it mean if a result is statistically significant but not biologically meaningful?
    It means the effect is real but very small — the large sample size made it statistically detectable, but the difference is too minor to matter biologically.

  2. Why do we say we “fail to reject” H₀ instead of “accepting” it?
    Because statistical tests can only show when data are inconsistent with H₀, not when H₀ is definitely true — there may simply be insufficient evidence to reject it.

  3. Why is correlation not the same thing as causation?
    Correlated variables may both change due to a third factor, coincidence, or indirect links; correlation shows association, not mechanism or cause.

  4. When might a one-tailed test be more appropriate than a two-tailed test? When you have a specific directional hypothesis, e.g. predicting a treatment will increase growth rather than just change it.

  5. What kind of data are suitable for a t-test compared with a chi-squared test?
    A t-test is for continuous, normally distributed data comparing means; a χ² test is for categorical count data comparing frequencies.

  6. What does a p-value actually represent in hypothesis testing?
    The probability of observing a result as extreme as (or more extreme than) your data if H₀ were true.

  7. Why should we check assumptions (like normality or independence) before running a test?
    Because violating assumptions can make p-values and confidence intervals inaccurate, leading to false conclusions.

  8. What kind of biological question would be best addressed using a correlation analysis?
    Questions about whether two continuous traits vary together, e.g. “Do larger animals tend to live longer?” or “Does enzyme activity increase with temperature?”
